\section{Dataset Analysis}

We used the subset of the city Meridian of the Yelp Open Dataset~\cite{yelp},
containing 1,045 businesses and 37,401 reviews. The custom tokenizer and
normalization described below produced 3,783,589 tokens and 38,120 distinct
terms. All frequency and vocabulary counts use this normalized text.

\subsection{Zipf's Law and Heaps' Law}

\subsubsection{Zipf's Law}
We counted how often each term appeared and ranked terms in descending order by frequency. Figure~\ref{p1:zipf} plots frequency against rank on
logarithmic axes. We fitted $f(r)=Cr^{-s}$ to ranks 10--10,000 using
least squares in log-space, giving $s=1.4695$ and $R^2=0.9869$.

Of the 38,120 distinct terms, 39.3\% appeared only once,
showing a clear long tail. Within the fitted range, frequency fell
faster than the classic $1/r$ relationship. The data therefore showed
Zipf-like behaviour, but did not follow the classic form exactly.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/part1_zipf.png}
  \caption{Term frequency by rank, with the fitted power law and a $1/r$ reference.}
  \Description{Log-log frequency-rank curve with many rare terms and a steeper tail.}
  \label{p1:zipf}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Heaps' Law}
We tracked vocabulary size $V$ as the token count $N$ increased.
We used 80 logarithmically spaced sample sizes, from 1,000 tokens
to the full corpus of 3,783,589 tokens.

Fitting $\log_{10}V=\log_{10}k+\beta\log_{10}N$ gave
$k=13.973$, $\beta=0.5356$ and $R^2=0.9922$ in log-space
(Figure~\ref{p1:heaps}). The vocabulary kept growing as more text was
added, but more slowly than the token count. The fitted exponent was
close to the typical value of 0.5.

To test prediction, we fitted the model again using only samples
containing at most 80\% of the full token count. It overestimated the
full vocabulary size by 26.2\%, as growth slowed more at larger sample
sizes than the model predicted. A high log-space $R^2$ therefore did
not guarantee an accurate vocabulary forecast.

\subsection{Custom Tokenizer}

\subsubsection{Rules}
We wrote rules using the \texttt{regex}
library
to identify words and numbers, split words at hyphens, and keep
complete emoji and text faces together.

For example, \texttt{don't}, \texttt{1,200.50} and \texttt{:-)}
are each kept as one token before normalization, while
\texttt{the-best-food} is split into \texttt{the}, \texttt{best}
and \texttt{food}. Listing~\ref{p1:scan} shows the main code.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Scanning the ordered regex alternatives.},label={p1:scan},basicstyle=\ttfamily\normalsize]
TOKEN_PATTERN = regex.compile(
    "|".join(f"(?P<{name}>{pattern})"
             for name, pattern in RULES),
    regex.VERSION1)
def scan(text):
    return [m.group() for m in
            TOKEN_PATTERN.finditer(text)
            if m.lastgroup != "DASH"]
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\par


Normalization applies NFC, standardizes apostrophes and converts
words to lowercase. Clear contractions are expanded, for example,
\texttt{don't} becomes \texttt{do} and \texttt{not}. Ambiguous forms
such as \texttt{she's} and \texttt{I'd} are not expanded.

Numbers, complete emoji and text faces are kept, while other
punctuation-only tokens are removed. Stopwords are retained,
without stemming or lemmatization.

\subsubsection{Comparison}
We compared the custom method with NLTK's Whitespace and WordPunct
tokenizers~\cite{nltk}, applying the same normalization after each method's
boundary decisions. Table~\ref{p1:vocab} reports their corpus-wide counts.
Whitespace retained punctuation-attached variants. WordPunct had the smallest
vocabulary, but split structures such as contractions and decimal numbers.
Vocabulary size alone does not measure output quality.

Table~\ref{p1:outputs} compares the outputs for a short constructed
example. Both Custom and Whitespace expand \texttt{don't}, while
WordPunct produces \texttt{don} and \texttt{t}. Custom also retains the
decimal number and emoji, and splits \texttt{well-made}. For this input,
the other methods lose the emoji during shared normalization because
it remains attached to the exclamation mark.
