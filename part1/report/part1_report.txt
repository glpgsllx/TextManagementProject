\section{Dataset Analysis}

We used the group's Meridian subset of the Yelp Open Dataset~\cite{yelp},
containing 1,045 businesses and 37,401 reviews. The custom tokenizer and
normalization described below produced 3,783,589 tokens and 38,120 distinct
terms. All frequency and vocabulary counts use this normalized text.

\subsection{Zipf's Law and Heaps' Law}

\subsubsection{Zipf's Law}
We counted each term's occurrences across the corpus, sorted the counts in
descending order and plotted frequency against rank on logarithmic axes
(Figure~\ref{p1:zipf}). We fitted $f(r)=Cr^{-s}$ by least squares in log-space
for ranks 10--10,000, excluding singleton counts. The fitted exponent was
$s=1.4695$, with $R^2=0.9869$ in log-space. Of the 38,120 terms, 14,964
(39.3\%) occurred only once. The curve therefore shows a strong long tail,
although its changing slope and exponent above one depart from the strict
$f(r)\propto 1/r$ relationship.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/part1_zipf.png}
  \caption{Term frequency by rank, with the fitted power law and a $1/r$ reference.}
  \Description{Log-log frequency-rank curve with many rare terms and a steeper tail.}
  \label{p1:zipf}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Heaps' Law}
We ordered reviews by a SHA-256 hash of seed 42 and the review identifier,
then read their tokens cumulatively. We measured vocabulary size $V$ at
80 logarithmically spaced token counts $N$, from 1,000 to 3,783,589.
Additional checkpoints marked 1k, 10k, 100k and 1M tokens and the
10\%, 30\%, 50\% and 100\% review samples. Together these gave 86 observations;
only the fixed 80-point grid entered the fit.

Least squares on $\log_{10}V=\log_{10}k+\beta\log_{10}N$ gave
$k=13.973$ and $\beta=0.5356$, with $R^2=0.9922$
(Figure~\ref{p1:heaps}). Vocabulary grew sublinearly, with an exponent close
to the typical value of 0.5. To test prediction, we refitted using only grid
points within 80\% of the full token count. These 77 points ended at
2,767,159 tokens. The model predicted 48,116 terms at full size, compared
with 38,120 observed, an overestimate of 26.2\%. The curve bends as growth
slows, so a close log-space fit across the full range does not ensure an
accurate vocabulary forecast.

\subsection{Custom Tokenizer}

\subsubsection{Rules}
We wrote ordered regex alternatives for complete emoji sequences, ASCII
emoticons, words with internal apostrophes, numbers, dash separators and
other non-whitespace characters. Emoji sequences include skin-tone modifiers,
flags, keycaps and zero-width-joiner combinations. Decimal points and commas
inside numbers are retained; hyphens separate words. Listing~\ref{p1:scan}
shows the core scan. The regex library supplies Unicode character
properties\footnote{\url{https://github.com/mrabarnett/mrab-regex}};
the token boundaries are defined by our rules.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Scanning the ordered regex alternatives.},label={p1:scan},basicstyle=\ttfamily\normalsize]
TOKEN_PATTERN = regex.compile(
    "|".join(f"(?P<{name}>{pattern})"
             for name, pattern in RULES),
    regex.VERSION1)
def scan(text):
    return [m.group() for m in
            TOKEN_PATTERN.finditer(text)
            if m.lastgroup != "DASH"]
\end{lstlisting}

Normalization applies NFC, unifies apostrophes, lowercases lexical tokens,
expands clear contractions and removes other punctuation-only tokens.
Ambiguous forms such as \texttt{she's} and \texttt{I'd} are retained.
Numbers, complete emoji and emoticons remain; stopwords are kept, without
stemming or lemmatization. Seventeen synthetic boundary cases, including complex emoji, were kept
separate from corpus statistics. Real emoji-rule matches were 13 text-style
symbols in six reviews.

\subsubsection{Comparison}
We compared the custom method with NLTK's Whitespace and WordPunct
tokenizers~\cite{nltk}, applying the same normalization after each method's
boundary decisions. Table~\ref{p1:vocab} reports their corpus-wide counts.
Whitespace retained punctuation-attached variants. WordPunct had the smallest
vocabulary, but split structures such as contractions and decimal numbers.
Vocabulary size alone does not measure output quality.

\end{table}

Listing~\ref{p1:outputs} compares the complete outputs for one unedited
sentence from a real review. A vertical bar separates tokens. Both Custom
and Whitespace expanded the complete \texttt{don't}. Custom also separated
the hyphenated words and retained \texttt{49.99} as one number. Whitespace
kept the hyphenated forms and the final period attached to the number;
WordPunct split the contraction into \texttt{don} and \texttt{t}, and the
number into \texttt{49} and \texttt{99}.
